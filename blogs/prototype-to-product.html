BUILDING / PRODUCT THINKING

The demo worked. The product is still a question.

Prasiddha Mainali · November 1, 2026 · 2 min read

DEMO ≠
DONETHE CURIOSITY LOG / PRASIDDHA MAINALI

A successful demo has a clear beginning, a prepared input, and someone nearby who knows which button to press. Real use is less considerate.

Someone opens the wrong file. Someone has a slow connection. Someone asks a reasonable question that never appeared in the project plan. These moments are not interruptions to the product. They are part of it.

Write the ordinary journey

Choose one person and one concrete task. What do they arrive with? What do they need to leave with? What is the smallest useful result the system can give them?

This exercise often makes a feature list shorter. A dashboard can contain twelve impressive charts and still fail to tell its reader what to do next. Removing a chart may improve the product more than adding a model.

Design the unglamorous states

A useful interface has something to say when there is no data, when a request fails, or when the answer is incomplete. “Something went wrong” is technically a sentence. It is rarely enough of an explanation.

Try writing those messages before writing the success message. It forces a surprisingly practical conversation about what the system actually knows.

Make the handoff real

Ask another person to try the core task without narration. Watch where they pause. Resist the urge to rescue every hesitation with “normally you would just…”

That sentence is a useful signal. It marks a piece of knowledge that lives in the builder’s head rather than in the product.

A prototype says, “Look what this can do.” A product also answers, “What should I do now?”

Keep the ambition; shrink the promise

Start with one dependable workflow. Explain its limits. Let the next feature follow a real need rather than the desire to make the diagram bigger.

There is plenty of room for ambitious engineering. It becomes more compelling when the person using it does not need to appreciate the engineering first.
