Introduction

Six months before NOAI, I had zero competition experience in AI. No structured prep, no idea what Olympiad-level preparation even looked like. Just a goal to compete with the best students in Nepal.

Somehow, it worked out.

💡

I didn't outwork everyone. I just didn't quit when it stopped feeling exciting.

The mistake that cost me weeks

Early on, I was learning from everywhere — YouTube playlists, AI influencer content, random blogs, course after course. It felt productive. It wasn't. I was consuming more than I was actually learning, and that fake sense of progress is dangerous because it's comfortable.

Real progress started the moment I stopped chasing resources and committed to going deeper into fewer things.

What the actual 6 months looked like

I wasn't grinding 10-hour days. Most days it was 1 to 1.5 hours, consistently, alongside college.

The first month or two felt painfully slow — Python, probability, linear algebra, basic ML. Overfitting, loss functions, gradients, bias-variance tradeoff. Nothing glamorous. But skipping this phase is exactly why most beginners hit a wall later. The fundamentals made everything else click faster.

After that, I stopped watching tutorials and started building. Image classification, CNNs, transfer learning, basic LSTMs, some computer vision work, even robotics with ESP32. I also reimplemented architectures from scratch — LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet. Not because NOAI explicitly tests these, but because building something from the ground up forces you to actually understand it. You can't fake your way through implementation.

No 10-hour grind sessions. No perfect schedule. Just showing up most days and doing the work

The mental side nobody talks about

There are students at these competitions who already publish research. Some have been coding for years, some compete internationally. If you measure yourself against them constantly, you'll quit.

What kept me going was one simple shift: just be slightly better than last week. That's it. Consistency compounds in ways motivation never does. There were plenty of days where progress felt invisible — concepts not clicking, college deadlines piling up, the whole thing feeling pointless. You push through anyway.

✅

Pro tip: Make your unit of measurement a week, not a day. Bad days will trick you into thinking you're not improving. A week never lies.

What I'd do differently

Start math earlier. Revise more instead of always moving forward. Build smaller projects more often instead of jumping to complex ones. And honestly — spend less time reading about AI and more time doing it.

The short version

Placing second wasn't about being the smartest person in the room. It was about showing up consistently for six months when it would've been easier not to.
